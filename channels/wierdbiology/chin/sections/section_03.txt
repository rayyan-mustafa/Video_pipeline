Now here's the part that broke the whole debate wide open.
In January 2026, anthropologist Noreen von Cramon-Taubadel and her team at the University of Buffalo published the most ambitious chin study ever attempted, in the journal PLOS One.
They didn't scan living volunteers.
They measured bone.
Five hundred and thirty-two actual skulls, pulled from museum collections across Europe and the United States.
Humans, stacked right alongside chimpanzees, gorillas, bonobos, gibbons, and orangutans.
On every single one, they mapped thirty-two precise landmarks.
And forty-six separate distances between them.
Then they ran it all through genetic simulations, built from an entire evolutionary family tree.
And here's the number that matters.
Of nine jaw traits tied to the chin, only three showed any real sign of direct evolutionary selection.
The other six — a full two-thirds — were just random chance.
Random.
Two out of every three chin-related traits, essentially inherited by accident.
Picture two buildings going up side by side, and in between them, a leftover triangular gap nobody actually designed on purpose.
Architects call that a spandrel.
Scientists have flagged other likely spandrels before — the human belly button, the shoulder hump on the extinct giant Irish deer, even the male-mimicking genitals of female hyenas.
Even elephants have something that looks like a chin, but it isn't one — just fused lip and trunk tissue left over from losing their front teeth.
And your own chin, this research suggests, is exactly that kind of leftover — bone debris, left behind when other parts of your skull changed shape around it.
The real action happened somewhere else entirely — in your upright walking posture, your ballooning brain, your shrinking teeth and jaws.
As those pieces rearranged themselves over hundreds of thousands of years, the front edge of your lower jaw simply had nowhere left to go but outward and down.
One biologist not involved in the study, James DiFrisco of the Francis Crick Institute, put it bluntly.
Just because something looks like one distinct "thing" doesn't mean evolution built it as one piece.
Let that sink in for a second.
But — and this is where it gets messy — that's not the only theory still standing.
Some researchers insist the chin does real mechanical work after all.
Gröning's own jaw-stress models found it genuinely reinforces the mandible against the strain of chewing.
Other scientists point somewhere stranger — toward sex itself.
Researchers Zaneta Thayer and Seth Dobson measured chin shape across nearly two hundred skulls, drawn from populations across the entire world.
They found a small but very real difference — men's chins run taller, more pronounced, than women's.
If the chin were purely about chewing or talking, they argued, both sexes should look basically the same down there.
They don't.
So three completely separate explanations — mechanical bracing, sexual signaling, and pure evolutionary accident — are all still fighting over the same three inches of bone.
Nobody has closed the case.
Which means the most identifiable feature on your entire face might not have been built for anything at all.
It might just be scar tissue from becoming human.
