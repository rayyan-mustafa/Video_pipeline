So here's where the whole story actually begins.
Back in 2007, a PhD researcher at the University of Otago named Ionut Ichim proposed something radical — that the chin might exist purely to brace the jaw against the stress of speech itself.
For decades before that, scientists barely took the chin seriously at all, treating it as biological junk — a useless leftover with no job to do.
Nobody had bothered to ask why.
No chimpanzee has one.
No gorilla, no orangutan, nothing else in our entire family tree.
Then everything changed.
Biological anthropologist Flora Gröning, at the University of York, built computer models comparing modern human jaws to actual Neanderthal jaws under real chewing loads.
Her models showed something nobody expected.
The chin actually helped the jaw survive those forces.
So what if this strange little bump wasn't some meaningless accident of appearance at all, but a hidden structural tool built to keep your skull from cracking under the pressure of everyday life?
It had been sitting under our mouths this whole time, waiting for someone to explain it.
